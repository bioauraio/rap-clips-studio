# Characters: model sheets, attributes, a hero who comes back

Thirty scenes drawn independently give you thirty different people. The character
system exists to stop that. It is the difference between a clip and a slideshow.

## Where they live

**Album characters** is a project-level panel, not a track-level one. Set a
character up once and they are available in every track of the album: frame
prompts use their name, and the looks are pulled in automatically.

**+ add character** opens a small dialog with two tabs. **New** creates one from
scratch. **From the library** copies a character out of any of your other
projects — name, description and reference photos come across. If you are building
a series, this is the tab you will use most.

![Character card: description of the looks, reference photos and attributes](/img/guide/characters.png)

## The description does the work

The field is **Personality and looks**, and its tooltip is blunt about what
happens to it: *goes into the prompts word for word.*

So write it as a description a stranger could draw from, not as a wish:

> Short, mid-thirties, shaved head, heavy brow, permanent squint. Grey tracksuit
> with a white stripe, black slides, a thin gold chain. Moves slowly, never smiles.

Not:

> A cool guy with a strong presence, iconic look, very charismatic.

The second one produces a different cool guy every scene. Adjectives about vibe
are not features a model can hold constant; a shaved head and a white stripe are.

Concrete beats poetic on three things especially: **hair, wardrobe, build.** Those
are what the eye uses to recognise a person across a cut.

## Photos versus a generated model sheet

Two ways to lock a face:

**+ reference photo** uploads your own images. Free. If you have a photo of the
person the clip is about, this is the better option — the face is real and it
stays real.

**Generate model sheet** draws a four-angle turnaround from your description and
whatever photos you have already uploaded. It costs 2 credits on the free and PRO
plans, 8 on PRO MAX and STUDIO, where it is drawn by a paid image engine that can
take many separate reference photos at once instead of one collage.

A turnaround is worth more than a single portrait: it gives the frame generator
the face from the side and the back, which is where one-photo characters usually
fall apart.

Practical order: upload two or three photos first, *then* generate the model
sheet. It uses them as reference, and you get a turnaround of your person rather
than an invented one.

## Attributes are separate on purpose

**Attributes** are the character's signature objects: a hat, glasses, a quad bike,
a specific jacket. They get their own name, their own description — which also
goes into the prompts — and their own photos.

They are separate from the character because they are not always in the frame. On
each scene card you toggle which characters are present, and which of their
attributes come with them. The helmet is in the riding scenes and not in the
kitchen scene, and the face stays the same in both.

Folding the quad bike into the character description instead means it appears in
the kitchen scene too. This is the single most common way a character setup goes
wrong.

Mark your lead as **main**. It is the one the story leans on when the model has to
choose whose scene this is.

## Why the same hero across clips

The five accounts we studied — 640+ videos between them — share one structural
habit: **their characters are franchises.** The same rat, the same monks, the same
recurring guy, in part II and part III. Not one clever video, a returning cast.

The numbers behind that habit are lopsided. In every account we measured, the top
ten videos took **44–68% of all views**, and the gap between the best video and
the median ran from 11× to 595×. You cannot aim at the hit. What you can do is
make the hit collectible: when one clip lands, a viewer who liked it has somewhere
to go, and the next clip starts from an audience instead of from zero.

A recurring character is what makes that possible. It is also, practically, the
cheapest thing in the studio: the description and the photos are already saved,
cloning them into the next project is one dialog, and it costs nothing.

## Common failures

**No character at all.** The story invents people, and they change every scene.
The one-click pipeline refuses to start for this reason — it wants a track with
audio, a style, and at least one named character.

**A description of a mood.** See above. Rewrite it as features.

**Too many characters.** Three named people in a thirty-scene clip is already a
lot to hold. Two is comfortable. One plus a crowd is often better than two.

**Attributes buried in the description.** Pull them out. That is what the
attribute list is for.

**A model sheet generated before any photos.** You get a stranger, and then you
spend the rest of the clip trying to make that stranger look like your idea.
